How it happened

You became the whole business.

Nobody planned it. You were good at the work, so you did the work. Then the selling. Then the invoices, the hiring, the late fixes and the posts at eleven at night. You learned every job because there was no one else to do it — and somewhere along the way, the business started running on your hours, your memory and your mood.

  • Your hours become the ceiling. The business can only grow as far as your week stretches.
  • Your judgement gets spent on small things, so the big ones are made tired, late, or not at all.
  • Your people wait for you, and slowly stop offering what they know.
  • Your price gets quoted from fear, because there is never time to work out what an hour of you is worth.
  • Your reason for starting gets buried under the next urgent thing.

Trying to master everything alone is not a strategy. It is a ceiling you built with good intentions.

Two kinds of stuck

You may be one of these on Monday, and the other by Friday.

A business that grows stands on three things at once: a reason you can say plainly, a strategy that says where and how, and the effort to do the work that matters. Miss one and the whole thing leans. Which one sounds like your week?

Why you can’t see it alone

One angle lies. Three don’t.

You are not blind. You are standing very close. Researchers call the cure triangulation: a finding you can trust is one that holds from more than one direction. Your feeling alone is loud and often wrong. Your numbers alone are quiet and miss the people. Someone outside doesn’t live your week. Put the three together and the real problem has nowhere left to hide.

      Be honest with yourself

      It is, if…

      • you are the founder, the owner, or the one everything still comes back to;
      • you are building, growing or scaling something real, with customers or close to them;
      • you can set aside time each week to look honestly, and do one real thing by Sunday;
      • you would rather see the truth than hear a pep talk.

      • you want a plan to admire rather than one you run;
      • you are looking for a tactic that works without you changing anything;
      • you want a course to watch. This is work you do, in your own business, with your own evidence.

      Questions founders ask

      Before you start.

      I don’t have time for a programme.

      That is usually the symptom, not the reason. Week one starts by counting where your hours actually went, so the first thing it gives you is time you can see. Each week asks for an honest look at your own evidence and one real thing done by Sunday — at your pace.

      Is this personal development or business?

      Both, in that order, and mostly business. The first of the four places to look is you, because in a small business the founder is the first system. The other three are your people, how the work runs, and your market — customers, price and pipeline.

      I’ve done courses before. Why would this be different?

      A course gives you ideas. Every step here sends you to something real — your calendar, your customers, your messages — and ends in a decision with a date. After week three, a person reads what you wrote. Nothing here can be finished by watching.

      I work alone. Is “leading people” for me?

      Yes. Your people are already there: the contractor, the partner, the client, the family member who covers for you. And the first person you lead is yourself, on a week when nobody is checking.
